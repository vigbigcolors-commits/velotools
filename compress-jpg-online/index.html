Why compress JPEG files?

Large JPG photos slow down websites, fill email inboxes, and hit upload limits on forms and marketplaces. A phone photo at 4000×3000 can be 4–8 MB straight from the camera — fine for archival, painful for a product page or a client proof email. Compressing before you publish keeps visual quality high while cutting bytes dramatically: better Core Web Vitals, faster LCP on image-heavy templates, and fewer “file too large” errors on portals that still insist on JPEG.

JPEG is a lossy format. That means you already trade some fidelity for size when the camera or phone saves the file. A second, controlled re-encode at a sensible quality (often 75–85%) usually removes redundant high-frequency detail humans barely notice, while recovering a large share of the file size. The goal is not maximum destruction — it is the smallest file that still looks intentional on the destination you care about.

Tips for best JPEG compression

  • 80–85% quality is the sweet spot for photos — smaller files, same look on screens.
  • For web, convert to WebP after compressing — often 25–35% smaller than JPEG at similar quality when the stack supports it.
  • Resize oversized camera photos to the display size you actually need before the final encode — a 2000px-wide hero does not need a 6000px master.
  • Already tiny JPEGs (<50KB) may not shrink much — they are already heavily compressed.
  • Screenshots with sharp UI text often look better as PNG or WebP; JPEG ringing shows on edges.
  • Need a marketplace square? Use a size preset such as Amazon or Etsy instead of guessing dimensions.

When to keep JPG vs switch formats

Keep JPEG when a portal, email client, or print vendor explicitly requires .jpg/.jpeg. Switch to WebP or AVIF for modern websites when you control the HTML and CDN — you will usually beat JPEG on bytes at the same perceived quality. Use PNG when you need lossless sharp edges or transparency. The compressor on VeloTools can output those formats from the same local pipeline so you are not bouncing files through a second upload tool.
